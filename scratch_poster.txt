Enhancing CuSCN’s Electrical Properties through Hydrogenation and Oxygenation 
for Solar Applications
Austin Giffen, Max Colomer, Steven J. Konezny
Departments of Physics and Chemistry, Energy Sciences Institute, Yale University, 810 West Campus Drive, New Haven, Connecticut, 06516, United States
Advancing renewable energy sources like solar is crucial in our fight against 
climate change and energy shortages that lead to widespread humanitarian 
crises. Solar devices specifically can be drastically improved through 
enhancing the hole transport layer (HTL) which facilitates electron hole 
movement out of the absorption layer and into the electrical load, reducing 
electron-hole recombination, subsequently improving device efficiency. 
Fig 1: Conventional solar cell model with hole movement and HTL shown in color
While poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrene sulfonate) is the most 
commonly employed HTL in solar cells today, it is not optimal for use in solar 
cells as it is not entirely transparent and detracts from the energy range of 
photons that can hit the absorption layer to be converted into energy. 
Additionally, it is very acidic and frequently traps electrons, reducing the 
number of holes that can facilitate charge transport over time. Copper(I) 
thiocyanate (CuSCN) has emerged as a promising alternative due to its low 
cost, high transparency, and superior hole mobility.

We built the Arduino–MFC control loop so the Arduino could both drive the MFCs to 
produce certain flow rates and read the MFC output to verify they were responding 
accurately. A DAC converted the Arduino’s control output into the analog signal the 
MFCs required and an ADC converted the analog output from the MFCs into a signal 
that the arduino could read. A chip select line was wired to each DAC to toggle between 
the devices so that only one device was active on the shared data bus at a time, allowing 
sequential addressing of each MFC without bus contention. 
Air, Nitrogen, and H₂/N₂ were each routed through an air-actuated valve manifold, 
which itself was controlled by a Nitrogen line that forked from the main Nitrogen line 
and was controlled by a pressure actuator. For high-precision conductivity 
measurements, we used an Agilent source-measure setup with four source-measure 
units (SMUs) and connected the SMU outputs to the gas-flow chamber using triaxial 
(triax) cable/guarded connections to minimize leakage and noise during low-current 
measurements. Occasionally, we wired environmental sensors (light, humidity, 
temperature, etc.) into some SMU channels, instead of devices.

We found that CuSCN samples left in ambient conditions for multiple 
months exhibited orders of magnitude increased conductivity.
- Grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) analysis showed no

- Placing samples in a vacuum yielded lower conductivity, ruling out 
evaporation
- XPS showed an increase in C=N double bonds and a decrease in 
C≡N triple bonds

We did not observe the 40% increase in conductivity that was achieved 
in previous literature with the same Hydrogen gas ratio
This could be due to various factors:
- Most likely device shape: 400 microns vs. 5 microns distance 
between electrodes
- There is not enough Au per surface of CuSCN to catalyze the 
reaction involving Hydrogen
- Gas flow rates: 4000 sccm vs. 500 sccm
- Volume of chamber: ~200mL  vs. 1L
